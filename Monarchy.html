The Whole Line

From Rome to Windsor

Britain had no kings of its own for the first four centuries of recorded history, and no single king of England until 927. Each step below is a handover: what changed, and who it went to. Click any one to zoom the reign map to that era.

How to read this. The map now runs from the Roman invasion of AD 43, so it begins with something that was not a monarchy at all: Britain was a province, governed in an emperor's name, and the Roman entries here are emperors, governors and one rebel queen rather than kings. Kingdom-making starts later — the Anglo-Saxons called no single man king of all England until Æthelstan in 927, and before that the land was divided between Kent, Sussex, Essex, East Anglia, Northumbria, Mercia and Wessex, all of whom are on the map with their own kings. The early eras and the Hanoverians are drawn in muted colours, so the four dynasties the map was built for — Plantagenet, Tudor, Stuart and Windsor — stay the vivid ones.

What this map does not show. It is the line of England and of the crown that inherited it, so the kings of Wales, Scotland and Ireland are outside it — apart from the Welsh and Scottish rulers who appear when English kings fought them. Some entries here are not monarchs at all: Roman officials, a rebel queen, semi-legendary founders, Danish client kings, and two women who ruled Mercia without a crown. Each says so in its own entry. Arthur is included because the question is always asked, and his entry states plainly that no contemporary source names him.

On the names. The "Stewards" are properly the Stuarts, from the French spelling of the Scottish surname Stewart, brought south when James VI inherited England in 1603. Between the Stuarts and the Windsors came the Hanoverians (1714–1901) and the short House of Saxe-Coburg-Gotha, renamed Windsor by George V in 1917 amid anti-German feeling in the First World War.

On dates. The Anglo-Saxon and Viking records are thin, and so are the dates. Where a reign is not securely known it is marked c. and the entry says what is uncertain. Names like the Unready, the Confessor or Bloody Mary are the real ones; a handful of others here are plainly descriptive rather than historical — nobody in the 1490s called Henry VII the Accountant King — and they are labelled as such so the record stays honest.
